We get called in for cloud cost reviews about once a quarter, and the request
is almost always framed the same way: "Our AWS bill is up 40% year-over-year.
Can you help us right-size?"

We usually can. But the right-sizing isn't what saves the money. The
**feedback loop** is.

## The shape of the problem

In every overrun engagement we've done, the technical root cause was different
— too many large EC2 instances, runaway egress, dev environments that nobody
turned off, log retention configured at the maximum. But the **organizational**
root cause was always the same.

The engineers making the decisions didn't see the bill. The finance team
seeing the bill didn't understand the decisions. So the cost grew quietly
until it became an alarm.

## What actually works

Three things, in order:

### 1. Tag everything, then tax everything

Every workload should be tagged with the team that owns it. Then those teams
should see a daily — not monthly — view of what their workloads cost. The
exact dollars don't have to be precise. The signal does.

### 2. Make the trade-off visible at the moment of decision

When an engineer is choosing between a managed database that costs $400/mo
and self-hosting one that costs $80, the right answer depends on the team's
operational maturity. But that conversation has to happen at the design
review, not at the next quarterly business review.

### 3. Stop treating the cloud bill like a fixed cost

In most organizations we walk into, the cloud bill is considered overhead.
That's a category error. The cloud bill is a direct, proportional consequence
of engineering decisions made every day. Treat it like a P&L line, with
owners.

## The boring conclusion

There's no exotic FinOps tool that fixes this. There's no architectural
pattern that, by itself, drops the bill 30%. There's just the work of
closing the feedback loop between the people who spend and the people
who pay. Every time we do that work, the savings show up. Every time we
skip it and just right-size, the bill is back in eighteen months.

The technology is the easy part. The organization always was.
